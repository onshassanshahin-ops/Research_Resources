2 · The dials

2%

Your best guess from the literature. If you have no idea, use 50% — it's the most cautious answer and gives the largest sample.

statisticians call this: expected proportion (p)
± 2 points

"Prevalence was 2%, give or take this much." Halving this roughly quadruples your sample.

statisticians call this: margin of error / half-width of the 95% CI
Total population, if you know it (optional)

Only helps when your population is small — sampling 400 out of 500 people is different from 400 out of a million.

30%

Your comparison group. Take this from a published study if you can, and cite it.

45%

Not what you hope for — the smallest difference that would actually change what someone does. This is the biggest lever on the whole calculation.

statisticians call this: the minimum clinically important difference
5 points

In the real units of your instrument. For the Zarit Burden Interview (0–88), a change of about 5 points is the usual threshold people treat as meaningful.

SD 14

The standard deviation, from a published study using the same instrument. Noisier measurements need bigger samples.

80%

80% is the usual minimum; 90% is safer and costs about a third more people.

statisticians call this: power (1 − β)
5%

5% is the convention. Lower it only if you have a reason you can defend.

statisticians call this: alpha (α), two-sided
1 : 1

Sometimes one group is easier to recruit. Unequal groups need more people in total.

3 · The two reality taxes

20%

People who don't answer, don't finish, or can't be reached. Beginners always underestimate this. 20–30% is normal for a survey.

1.5

People from the same place resemble each other, so each extra person from that place tells you less. 1.0 means you're sampling individuals independently; 1.5–2.0 is typical for clinic or school clusters.

statisticians call this: the design effect (DEFF)

What the four dials mean

The difference you care about

The smaller the difference you want to catch, the more people you need — and it grows fast. This is the dial students never think about and the one that decides everything.

How noisy the measurement is

A shaky instrument or a varied population hides real differences. More noise, more people.

Power

Your chance of finding a real difference if one is genuinely there. At 80% power you are accepting a one-in-five chance of missing it.

Alpha

Your tolerance for calling something real when it isn't. Conventionally 5%.

And then reality

People drop out, and people recruited from the same clinic resemble each other. Both mean you recruit more than the textbook number.
